  • Quantum Defense Optimization

An INSTAR Lab research direction: applying quantum optimization algorithms to the combinatorial scheduling, routing, and tasking problems that classical solvers find intractable at mission scale

Defense planning confronts a class of problems that scales combinatorially: assigning sensor assets across a theater, sequencing logistics convoys through contested routes, scheduling maintenance windows across a distributed fleet, or allocating electromagnetic spectrum in a congested environment. Classical algorithms handle modest versions of these problems well, but operational scale (hundreds of assets, thousands of constraints, compressed time windows) drives solution times beyond what tactical decision cycles permit. Heuristics help, but they trade optimality for speed in ways that may matter when margins are tight.

Quantum computing offers a structurally different approach to this problem class. Rather than searching solution spaces sequentially, quantum algorithms exploit superposition and interference to evaluate multiple candidate solutions simultaneously. Current noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) hardware is still far from fault-tolerant at the scale needed for live operational use, but the research trajectory is clear, and the window for establishing foundational competency is now. INSTAR Lab (Ravonics' affiliated 501(c)(3) research bench) is pursuing this as a research direction targeting future SBIR/STTR engagement, not a shipping product. The sections below describe the conceptual pillars shaping that inquiry.

  1. The Defense Optimization Problem Class

    The problems of interest are combinatorial: they involve selecting, ordering, or assigning discrete items under constraints, with an exponentially large solution space. Canonical forms include vehicle routing problems, traveling-salesman variants, quadratic unconstrained binary optimization (QUBO), and constraint-satisfaction problems. Defense instantiations include multi-UAV waypoint planning, ISR sensor retasking, supply route deconfliction, and communications frequency assignment. Each shares the property that classical exact solvers become intractable as instance size grows, making them natural targets for quantum speedup research.

  2. Gate-Model vs. Quantum Annealing Approaches

    Two hardware paradigms are relevant. Gate-model quantum computers (IBM, Google, IonQ) execute sequences of quantum logic gates on qubits: general-purpose, but currently limited to shallow circuits by noise. Quantum annealers (D-Wave) are purpose-built for QUBO problems, mapping the cost function directly onto physical qubit couplings and allowing the system to relax toward a minimum-energy configuration. Annealers can handle larger instances today on their native problem class, but gate models promise broader applicability as hardware matures. INSTAR Lab's research evaluates both paradigms against defense-relevant problem encodings.

  3. Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA)

    QAOA is the leading candidate gate-model algorithm for combinatorial optimization on near-term hardware. It encodes the optimization objective as a cost Hamiltonian and applies alternating cost and mixer unitaries, parameterized by angles that are classically tuned to steer the quantum state toward good solutions. Depth (number of rounds, denoted p) trades circuit complexity against solution quality; shallow circuits are NISQ-feasible but yield approximate results. Understanding where QAOA outperforms classical approximation algorithms, and for which problem structures, is a key open question in the research community and a focal point for INSTAR Lab's benchmarking work.

  4. Hybrid Classical-Quantum Architectures

    Full quantum solutions to large defense problems are not feasible on near-term hardware. The practical near-term model is hybrid: classical processors handle problem decomposition, parameter optimization, and result post-processing, while the quantum processor is invoked for the computationally intensive subproblem where quantum advantage is expected to emerge. Variational quantum eigensolvers and quantum-classical feedback loops exemplify this pattern. Designing effective decompositions (knowing which subproblems benefit from quantum treatment and which are better left classical) is itself a research contribution that defense-domain expertise informs directly.

  5. NISQ Realism and Error Considerations

    Current quantum hardware is noisy: gate errors, decoherence, and readout errors accumulate with circuit depth, degrading result quality. NISQ-era research must account for this honestly. Error mitigation techniques (zero-noise extrapolation, probabilistic error cancellation, symmetry verification) extend the useful depth of near-term circuits but add classical overhead. Fault-tolerant quantum computing, which would enable deep circuits and strong guarantees, requires logical qubits built from many physical qubits and remains a medium-to-long horizon. INSTAR Lab's research posture acknowledges these constraints and targets problem formulations and circuit depths appropriate to demonstrated hardware capability.

  6. Near-Term vs. Long-Term Research Horizon

    Near-term work focuses on problem encoding, benchmark development, and hybrid architecture design: contributions that accrue regardless of when fault-tolerant hardware arrives and position the team to exploit improved hardware as it becomes available. Medium-term milestones include demonstrating quantum advantage on small, defense-relevant benchmark instances. Long-term, fault-tolerant hardware opens the door to Grover-style unstructured search and quantum speedups on constraint-satisfaction problems at operationally relevant scale. Ravonics and INSTAR Lab are building toward this trajectory through SBIR/STTR research pathways, not claiming current product capability.
