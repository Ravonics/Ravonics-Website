A Ravonics perspective on why interoperability (not any single platform) is the hard problem in multi-domain operations

JADC2 sets out to connect sensors and decision-makers across every operational domain (air, land, sea, space, and cyber) so that the right data reaches the right node at the speed of relevance. The vision is not a new radio or a new application; it is a network of networks in which any sensor can inform any effect, regardless of which service, program, or vendor fielded it.

Our thesis is that the hard problem is not any one platform; it is interoperability and data. Connecting heterogeneous systems that were never designed to talk to one another, reconciling their data models, and moving information across accreditation boundaries is where multi-domain integration succeeds or fails. New entrants add value precisely by being integration-disciplined and standards-first rather than by fielding yet another stovepipe to be bridged later.

  1. Data-Centric Architecture

    Decouple data from the applications that produce and consume it. When information lives in a shared, addressable data layer rather than locked inside point solutions, new capabilities subscribe to what already exists instead of demanding bespoke integrations. This inverts the traditional application-centric model and is the structural prerequisite for any sensor informing any effect across a federation of systems.

  2. Open Standards & Common Data Models

    Interoperability at scale depends on published, vendor-neutral interfaces and shared data models, not proprietary connectors negotiated one pair at a time. Adopting open message formats, common schemas, and documented APIs lets independently developed systems exchange information without custom glue. Standards-first design also protects the program from lock-in and keeps the integration surface auditable over a long acquisition lifecycle.

  3. Semantic Interoperability

    A shared format is necessary but not sufficient; systems must also share meaning. A track, a unit, or a time reference has to carry the same definition across every participating system, or fused pictures quietly diverge. Ontologies, controlled vocabularies, and explicit unit and reference conventions give data unambiguous interpretation, so that machines, not just operators, can reason over information that crossed a domain boundary.

  4. Event-Driven Mesh & Publish/Subscribe

    Multi-domain C2 favors loosely coupled, event-driven messaging over brittle request/response chains. A publish/subscribe mesh lets producers emit information once and any authorized consumer react, with no producer needing to know its consumers in advance. This pattern scales as nodes join and leave, tolerates partial connectivity, and keeps the architecture open to capabilities that were not anticipated when the system was first fielded.

  5. Cross-Domain & Multi-Level Security

    Moving data between networks of different sensitivity requires deliberate, accredited separation rather than ad hoc bridges. One-way data guards, controlled interfaces, and clear accreditation boundaries let information flow where policy permits while enforcing the direction and content of every transfer. The design goal is principled data movement that respects security boundaries by construction, not by exception, and never by assumption.

  6. Test, Observability & Graceful Degradation

    A federation is only as trustworthy as its weakest link, so integration must be continuously tested and instrumented end to end. Contract testing, distributed tracing, and health telemetry across federated systems surface faults before an operator does. Just as important is graceful degradation: when a node, link, or feed drops, the wider picture should narrow predictably rather than fail outright, preserving usable command and control under stress.
