CMMC 2.0: What Defense Contractors Must Understand Before First Award | Ravonics

CMMC 2.0 explained for defense contractors: Level 1/2/3 requirements, NIST SP 800-171 alignment, self-assessment vs C3PAO, CUI handling, SPRS scoring, and POA&M. Ravonics is itself pursuing this pathway as a new small business.

A Ravonics perspective on what the CMMC 2.0 framework actually requires, written from the same vantage point as every new defense contractor pursuing the pathway for the first time

The Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification (CMMC) 2.0 program became a contractual reality with the publication of the 48 CFR rule in late 2024. For defense contractors handling Federal Contract Information (FCI) or Controlled Unclassified Information (CUI), the pathway to award on DoW contracts increasingly runs through a demonstrated, documented, and in many cases independently assessed cybersecurity posture. The program is not new in intent (DFARS 252.204-7012 has required NIST SP 800-171 compliance since 2017), but CMMC 2.0 introduces formal verification requirements that self-attestation alone can no longer satisfy for higher-level contracts.

Ravonics is itself pursuing this pathway as a new small business. Our NIST 800-171 alignment is underway and our CMMC assessment is planned. We hold no CMMC certification today. What we can offer is a clear-eyed account of what the framework actually requires, drawn from firsthand engagement with the assessment preparation process and the technical demands of NIST SP 800-171 implementation.

  1. Understanding the Three Levels and Which Applies to You

    CMMC 2.0 collapsed the original five-level model into three. Level 1 covers 17 basic safeguarding practices from FAR 52.204-21 and applies to contractors handling only FCI (not CUI). Level 2 aligns exactly to the 110 practices in NIST SP 800-171 and applies to contractors handling CUI; most DoW contracts with CUI flow-down will require Level 2. Level 3 adds 24 practices drawn from NIST SP 800-172 for contractors on the highest-priority programs. Determining which level applies starts with whether your work involves CUI and whether any solicitation clause (notably DFARS 252.204-7021) specifies a required level. Assuming Level 1 applies to any program touching DoW data is a risk posture that warrants reassessment.

  2. Self-Assessment vs. C3PAO: Who Assesses You

    Level 1 and some Level 2 contracts allow annual self-assessment, where a senior company official affirms compliance in the Supplier Performance Risk System (SPRS). Level 2 contracts designated as "critical" by the program office require a triennial third-party assessment by a CMMC Third Party Assessment Organization (C3PAO) certified by the CMMC Accreditation Body (Cyber AB). Level 3 requires a government-led assessment by DCSA. Contractors should identify early whether their target contract vehicle will require a C3PAO assessment, because the scheduling backlog and cost of a third-party assessment are material planning inputs, not last-minute concerns.

  3. NIST SP 800-171: The 110 Controls Beneath Level 2

    NIST SP 800-171 Revision 2 defines 110 security requirements across 14 domains: access control, awareness and training, audit and accountability, configuration management, identification and authentication, incident response, maintenance, media protection, personnel security, physical protection, risk assessment, security assessment, system and communications protection, and system and information integrity. Revision 3 (published 2024) reorganizes and adds requirements; contractors should monitor which revision their assessment will reference. Each requirement must be implemented, documented, and demonstrable; "we plan to implement" is not compliant unless accompanied by a properly scoped Plan of Action and Milestones.

  4. SPRS Score, POA&M, and the 110-Point System

    SPRS (Supplier Performance Risk System) hosts a contractor's self-assessed score against the NIST SP 800-171 DoW Assessment Methodology. A perfect score is 110; each unimplemented practice carries a weighted deduction, with the most critical controls reducing the score the most steeply. A score below 110 must be accompanied by a Plan of Action and Milestones (POA&M) documenting the gap, the remediation approach, and the expected completion date. The SPRS score is visible to contracting officers and is a factor in contract award decisions. Contractors who submit a score without a credible, time-bounded POA&M for each gap are exposed to False Claims Act risk if the score is later found to have been submitted without a good-faith basis.

  5. CUI Scoping and Enclave Architecture

    One of the most consequential decisions in CMMC preparation is defining the CUI boundary: the scope of systems, people, and processes that touch Controlled Unclassified Information. A broader CUI boundary means more systems in scope for the 110 controls, more devices to harden, more endpoints to monitor, and higher assessment cost. Many contractors reduce this burden through CUI enclave architecture: isolating CUI handling to a defined, hardened environment (dedicated endpoints, segmented network, strict access control) while keeping the broader business environment out of scope. The enclave must be technically enforced and consistently maintained; inspectors assess the actual environment, not the diagram.

  6. Flow-Down, Subcontractors, and Supply Chain Responsibility

    CMMC requirements flow down to subcontractors who handle CUI or FCI on the same program. A prime contractor who achieves Level 2 certification cannot satisfy program requirements if a subcontractor handling CUI operates without equivalent controls. The prime is responsible for including DFARS 252.204-7021 in subcontracts and for verifying that subcontractors have the required certification prior to award. Small businesses entering the defense supply chain as subcontractors should not assume CMMC is only the prime's problem; flow-down clauses make it everyone's obligation. Building the assessment and certification timeline into subcontract negotiations, rather than after task order award, avoids the most common schedule surprise.
