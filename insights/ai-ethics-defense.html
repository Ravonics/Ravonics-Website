AI Ethics in Defense: Autonomy, Accountability, and the Human-on-the-Loop Principle

  • Insights
  • Industry Perspectives
  • AI Ethics in Defense

A Ravonics defense AI policy perspective on how DoW's ethical AI framework and the human-on-the-loop principle are reshaping what responsible AI procurement actually requires

Defense AI is moving from research laboratory to acquisition program at a pace that has outrun the policy infrastructure designed to govern it. DoW's five ethical AI principles (responsible, equitable, traceable, reliable, and governable) were articulated in 2020 and affirmed through the Responsible AI Strategy and Implementation Pathway. DoW Directive 3000.09, updated in 2023, sets the policy framework for autonomous weapon systems and the human judgment requirements that must accompany them. Yet the gap between policy language and program-level implementation remains wide, and acquisition programs that do not engage with that gap early are increasingly encountering friction at test and evaluation, milestone reviews, and Congressional oversight.

The ethical AI conversation in defense is not primarily a philosophical one; it is an acquisition policy conversation. The questions that matter at program level are concrete: What level of human control is required for this system's intended use? What does the program office need to demonstrate to satisfy DoW Directive 3000.09 review requirements? How is accountability assigned when an AI-enabled decision leads to an unintended outcome? What does "traceable" mean in practice for a machine learning model that produces recommendations under time pressure? The following six dimensions frame the policy landscape that defense AI programs must navigate in 2026 and beyond.

  1. Human-on-the-Loop vs. Human-in-the-Loop: The Distinction That Matters

    The distinction between human-in-the-loop and human-on-the-loop control is not semantic; it is the organizing axis of DoW's autonomy policy. Human-in-the-loop means a human must affirmatively authorize each consequential action; the system cannot act without that authorization in the decision cycle. Human-on-the-loop means the system can act autonomously within defined parameters, but a human retains the ability to monitor, intervene, and override. DoW Directive 3000.09 governs which category applies to which class of autonomous and semi-autonomous weapon systems, and the classification has direct implications for what legal review, operational concept approval, and commander accountability a program requires. Blurring this distinction in program documentation (describing a human-on-the-loop system as having "human oversight" without specifying the actual control architecture) is the single most common source of friction at 3000.09 legal and policy reviews.

  2. DoW's Five Ethical AI Principles as Acquisition Requirements

    DoW's ethical AI principles are not aspirational statements; they translate into testable acquisition requirements when a program team is serious about them. "Responsible" requires that a designated human be accountable for AI development and employment decisions. "Equitable" requires testing for bias across the populations and operating conditions the system will encounter. "Traceable" requires that the system's methodology be auditable and its outputs explicable to the humans relying on them. "Reliable" requires that the system perform within defined boundaries under expected and unexpected conditions. "Governable" requires that the system can be deactivated, adjusted, or overridden. Each of these generates specific test and evaluation requirements that must be scoped into a program's T&E strategy well before milestone B if they are not to become schedule-breaking late discoveries.

  3. Accountability Chains Cannot Be Diffused by Algorithmic Complexity

    A persistent challenge in defense AI acquisition is the tendency for accountability to become diffuse when a machine learning model sits between a human decision-maker and an outcome. Vendors sometimes implicitly rely on algorithmic opacity to limit their liability exposure. Program offices sometimes accept this opacity because it makes oversight easier to defer. Neither posture is legally or operationally sustainable. The law of armed conflict imposes accountability on commanders for actions taken by systems under their control. That accountability cannot be delegated to an algorithm. Programs that design accountability chains from the outset (who is responsible for training data quality, who approves the operational parameters, who has authority to suspend the system) are better positioned at legal reviews and better equipped to investigate and learn from incidents in the field.

  4. Test and Evaluation Must Include Adversarial and Out-of-Distribution Conditions

    Machine learning systems can perform with high measured accuracy on evaluation datasets while failing in ways that are unexpected and potentially consequential when they encounter inputs outside their training distribution. Defense operating environments are specifically designed by adversaries to create out-of-distribution conditions: sensor spoofing, environmental camouflage, electronic warfare, novel platform signatures. A T&E strategy for a defense AI system that evaluates only nominal performance against representative datasets is providing false confidence. The Responsible AI (RAI) guidelines from CDAO and the operational test and evaluation community increasingly expect programs to demonstrate adversarial robustness, failure mode characterization, and performance bounds across the full operational envelope, not just the favorable test conditions that maximize reported accuracy.

  5. Traceability and Explainability Are Not the Same Requirement

    DoW's principle of traceability addresses two related but distinct needs that programs often conflate. Audit traceability means being able to reconstruct what data the model was trained on, what parameters were set, what version was deployed, and what inputs produced a specific output: the forensic record needed to investigate a decision after the fact. Explainability addresses the real-time need: can a human decision-maker understand why the system produced a recommendation well enough to exercise informed judgment about whether to act on it? These requirements differ in who needs them (auditors versus operators), when they are needed (post-hoc versus real-time), and what technical investment they require. Programs that design only for audit traceability while leaving operators without actionable explanations of system outputs are not meeting the intent of the principle.

  6. Responsible AI Is Becoming a Competitive Differentiator in Defense Procurement

    Congressional attention to autonomous systems, CDAO's expanding role in AI governance, and the growing sophistication of program executive offices in evaluating AI vendor claims are collectively raising the bar for what responsible AI means in a defense procurement context. Vendors who can demonstrate mature responsible AI practices (documented accountability chains, adversarial T&E results, explainability tooling appropriate to operator cognitive loads, and credible human control architectures) are increasingly distinguishable from those offering capable models without governance substance. For a new small business like Ravonics, positioning on responsible AI practice is not a hedge against capability claims; it is an honest reflection of the architectural discipline that federal AI programs actually require from solution providers.
