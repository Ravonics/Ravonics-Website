JADC2 Multi-Domain Integration: Why Interoperability Is the Hard Problem | Ravonics

Why the real JADC2 challenge is not bandwidth but semantic interoperability across service and domain data silos, and the strategic choices that determine whether multi-domain C2 integration succeeds or stalls.

A Ravonics industry perspective on why JADC2's defining challenge is semantic interoperability across service silos (not bandwidth or processing speed)

Joint All-Domain Command and Control is the defining C2 modernization program of the current defense era, and the ambition is genuinely transformative: sense, make sense, and act across land, sea, air, space, and cyberspace faster than any adversary can respond. The program has attracted substantial investment, generated a crowded ecosystem of data-link and cloud-native integration proposals, and produced important technical work at each service's program office. Yet the hardest problem remains stubbornly unsolved, and it is not the one most vendors are selling solutions to.

Bandwidth, latency, and compute power are real constraints, but they are tractable engineering problems with established solution patterns. The constraint that actually determines whether JADC2 produces a coherent joint picture or a cacophony of incompatible service feeds is semantic interoperability: whether a track generated by a Navy sensor and a target record produced by an Air Force ISR platform mean the same thing when they arrive at a joint C2 node. Without a shared data model, all the transport capacity in the world delivers unresolvable ambiguity at the decision layer. The following perspective examines the six strategic dimensions that separate programs that achieve genuine multi-domain fusion from those that produce integration theater.

  1. The Semantic Gap Is the Core Problem

    Each military service has evolved its own data models, message formats, and ontologies over decades of independent system development. A "track" in one service's lexicon carries different mandatory fields, confidence scoring, and provenance metadata than a nominally equivalent record in another's. When these heterogeneous feeds arrive at a joint fusion engine, the system faces a choice: force a lowest-common-denominator schema that loses critical context, or maintain parallel representations that defeat the purpose of fusion. Neither outcome is acceptable at the speed multi-domain operations demand. Closing the semantic gap requires agreement on a common data model (not a translation layer, but a shared meaning layer) that each domain's data is mapped into before it enters the joint fabric.

  2. Data Fabric Architecture Over Point-to-Point Integration

    The instinct of many integration programs is to build bilateral connectors: translate System A's output into System B's input. This approach scales as O(n²): each new node added to the joint network requires new connectors to every existing node. A data fabric architecture inverts this: each system publishes to and subscribes from a shared semantic layer governed by common schemas and access policies. New participants write one adapter to the fabric, not dozens of bilateral integrations. The architectural choice between connector sprawl and a fabric model is a program-level strategic decision that determines long-term maintainability and the cost of adding new joint partners.

  3. Standards Adoption Is a Policy Problem, Not a Technical One

    Common data model standards for defense (NATO STANAG 5516, USMTF, emerging JADC2 data standards from CDAO) exist or are in active development. The technical content is generally sound. The barrier to adoption is not schema quality; it is service-level program sovereignty. Each service's program office controls its own acquisition budget and schedule, and conforming to a joint standard imposes cost and schedule risk that falls on one program while the benefit accrues jointly. Resolving this requires acquisition policy mechanisms: contract data requirements lists that mandate schema conformance, DoW Chief Digital and AI Office (CDAO) governance with enforcement authority, and joint requirements that make interoperability a first-class acceptance criterion rather than an integration test afterthought.

  4. Cross-Domain Security Is a First-Order Architectural Constraint

    Multi-domain C2 necessarily spans classification boundaries. A joint common operational picture that fuses intelligence community feeds with unclassified partner-nation data must enforce access control at the attribute level, not just at the network perimeter. Cross-domain solutions (CDS) that guard data flows between classification enclaves introduce latency, impose schema constraints, and create single points of failure. Architects who treat cross-domain security as an afterthought (a gateway to bolt on once the fusion architecture is designed) consistently discover that the CDS requirements reshape the entire data model. Cross-domain policy must inform schema design from the beginning, not be retrofitted to an architecture that assumed a single-domain world.

  5. Disconnected and Degraded Operations Require Edge Coherence

    JADC2's vision of a continuously fused joint picture assumes some level of connectivity. Real multi-domain operations include periods of denied, degraded, intermittent, and limited (DDIL) communications where individual nodes lose access to the joint fabric entirely. The interoperability architecture must define behavior during disconnected operations: what local state is authoritative, how conflicts are resolved on reconnect, and what minimum data set a node needs to operate autonomously. Programs that design only for the connected case build a system that degrades gracefully in the lab and catastrophically in the field. Edge coherence is an interoperability requirement, not a communications requirement.

  6. Measuring Interoperability Requires Joint Test Events

    Interoperability cannot be self-certified. A service that tests its JADC2 interface against its own message simulator is measuring compliance with its own assumptions, not actual joint compatibility. The only credible measure is a joint test event where heterogeneous systems operated by different organizations exchange live data under realistic mission scenarios and adjudicate disagreements at the schema level, not at the "it worked in the demo" level. Programs that reach this standard expose the semantic gaps early, when they are fixable. Programs that defer joint testing until late-stage integration typically surface incompatibilities that are expensive to fix and politically difficult to acknowledge. Genuine interoperability is demonstrated in adversarial joint exercises, not vendor showcases.
