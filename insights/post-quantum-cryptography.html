Post-Quantum Cryptography: Preparing Federal Communications | Ravonics

Why federal systems should begin migrating now: the harvest-now-decrypt-later threat, NIST FIPS 203/204/205 standards (ML-KEM, ML-DSA, SLH-DSA), cryptographic agility, and a phased hybrid transition. A Ravonics architectural primer.

A Ravonics architectural primer on protecting today's secrets against tomorrow's quantum adversary

The public-key cryptography securing federal communications today rests on mathematical problems that classical computers cannot solve in any practical time. A sufficiently capable quantum computer changes that calculus: Shor's algorithm would break the RSA and elliptic-curve schemes underpinning TLS, VPNs, code signing, and key exchange across nearly every federal system. The timeline for such a machine remains uncertain, but the risk window has already opened.

The reason is a threat model called "harvest now, decrypt later." A capable adversary does not need a quantum computer today to benefit from one tomorrow. They need only to capture encrypted traffic now and store it. When a cryptographically relevant quantum computer arrives, that archived traffic is retroactively exposed. For federal communications (diplomatic cables, intelligence reporting, classified program data, and personnel records with decades-long sensitivity) the secret you transmit today may still demand protection long after quantum decryption becomes feasible. That mismatch is why the migration must begin now, not when the threat fully materializes.

  1. The Harvest-Now-Decrypt-Later Threat

    Adversaries are already recording encrypted federal traffic and archiving it against a future decryption capability. The governing question is not when a quantum computer arrives, but how long your data must stay secret. Where that lifetime exceeds the migration window plus the adversary's likely time-to-capability, the secret is effectively at risk today, and waiting only widens the exposure.

  2. The New NIST Standards

    In 2024 NIST finalized its first post-quantum standards: FIPS 203 specifies ML-KEM (formerly Kyber) for key encapsulation, FIPS 204 specifies ML-DSA (formerly Dilithium) for digital signatures, and FIPS 205 specifies SLH-DSA (formerly SPHINCS+), a stateless hash-based signature with conservative security assumptions. These lattice- and hash-based schemes resist known quantum attacks and form the foundation for compliant federal migration.

  3. Cryptographic Inventory & Dependency Mapping

    You cannot migrate what you cannot see. The first practical step is a thorough inventory of every place cryptography lives: protocols, libraries, certificates, hardware modules, embedded firmware, and third-party services. Mapping these dependencies surfaces hidden uses, quantifies data sensitivity and lifetime, and produces the prioritized, risk-ranked basis for any credible transition plan.

  4. Cryptographic Agility

    Hard-coding a single algorithm is what made the current transition so painful. Cryptographic agility abstracts algorithms behind stable interfaces, so primitives can be swapped through configuration and policy rather than re-engineering. Designing for agility now means the next standard (or an emergency response to a broken scheme) becomes a managed change rather than a multi-year forklift across the enterprise.

  5. Hybrid / Phased Transition

    Federal migration favors hybrid schemes that run a classical algorithm and a post-quantum algorithm in parallel, combining both into a single key exchange. The session stays secure as long as either holds, hedging against an immature PQC implementation while delivering quantum resistance immediately. A phased rollout (beginning with the highest-sensitivity, longest-lived channels) contains risk and builds operational confidence.

  6. Governance, Testing & Roadmap Alignment

    Migration is a governance program, not a one-time patch. Align planning with NSA's CNSA 2.0 timelines and federal guidance, establish interoperability and regression testing for hybrid stacks, and validate against the standardized parameter sets. Clear ownership, measurable milestones, and a maintained roadmap keep the effort on track as standards mature and agency mandates take effect.
