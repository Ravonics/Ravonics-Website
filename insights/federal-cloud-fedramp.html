Federal Cloud Adoption: What FedRAMP-Aligned Architecture Actually Requires

  • Insights
  • Industry Perspectives
  • Federal Cloud & FedRAMP

A Ravonics design-first perspective on FedRAMP-aligned cloud architecture, and an honest accounting of what the framework actually demands before an agency workload moves to the cloud

Federal cloud adoption has accelerated since the 2017 Cloud Smart strategy and the OMB mandate requiring agencies to move workloads off legacy infrastructure. Yet the gap between "cloud-hosted" and "FedRAMP-authorized" remains wider than many program managers expect. The Federal Risk and Authorization Management Program (FedRAMP) is not a checkbox; it is a sustained engineering and governance commitment that begins at the architecture drawing board, not at the authorization package stage.

Ravonics holds no FedRAMP authorization. What we bring is design-first architectural guidance: structuring cloud environments from the start so that the path to authorization (whether pursued by the agency's CSP or by the program itself) is navigable rather than remediated under deadline. The six considerations below reflect the structural choices that determine whether a cloud architecture is genuinely FedRAMP-aligned or simply cloud-hosted.

  1. Authorization Boundary Definition

    Every FedRAMP package begins with a precise authorization boundary: the documented scope of what is (and is not) covered by the security assessment. Agencies and cloud service providers routinely struggle here because modern cloud architectures blur the edges. Data flows traverse microservices, third-party APIs, and shared platform layers. Defining the boundary tightly enough to be assessable while broadly enough to cover the actual threat surface is the first and most consequential architectural decision. Boundary creep discovered late in the assessment cycle is among the most common causes of authorization delay.

  2. Impact Level Selection (Low / Moderate / High, IL2–IL6)

    FedRAMP's impact levels (Low, Moderate, and High, mapped to DoW's IL2 through IL6 for defense workloads) determine the NIST SP 800-53 control baseline the system must satisfy. Most civilian agency workloads qualify for Moderate; defense workloads carrying CUI or mission-sensitive data may require High or IL4/IL5. Selecting the wrong level early forces either a costly re-architecture or an underpowered security posture. The decision should be driven by the data classification that will ever transit the system, not by what is convenient today.

  3. Control Inheritance and the Shared Responsibility Model

    FedRAMP's power is its inheritance model: an already-authorized CSP passes a defined set of controls downstream to agency tenants, reducing the assessment burden. But inheritance is not automatic; it must be explicitly documented, scoped to the specific service configuration in use, and verified against the CSP's current System Security Plan. Controls that are "inherited" in name but differ in implementation between the CSP's assessed environment and the agency's deployment create audit findings. Understanding exactly which controls are fully inherited, partially inherited, and fully agency-owned is foundational work that cannot be deferred.

  4. Continuous Monitoring (ConMon) Architecture

    FedRAMP authorization is not a one-time event. The Continuous Monitoring program requires monthly vulnerability scanning, annual penetration testing, Plan of Action and Milestones (POA&M) management, and regular reporting to the authorizing official. Agencies that design their cloud environments without instrumenting for ConMon from day one find themselves retrofitting logging pipelines, SIEM integrations, and scan tooling under operational pressure. Continuous monitoring should be a first-class design constraint alongside availability and cost, not an afterthought.

  5. Identity, Access, and PIV/CAC Integration

    Federal identity requirements do not relax in the cloud. HSPD-12, PIV/CAC authentication, and the phishing-resistant MFA requirements in OMB M-22-09 apply regardless of hosting environment. Cloud architectures that rely on username-and-password federation as a temporary measure routinely carry that debt into the authorization assessment. Building PIV/CAC-compatible identity providers, enforcing attribute-based access control, and logging every privileged session to a tamper-evident store are baseline expectations, not advanced features.

  6. Data Residency, Encryption, and Key Management

    Federal data at rest and in transit requires FIPS 140-2/140-3 validated cryptographic modules: a requirement that rules out default cloud encryption configurations that use non-validated libraries. Beyond algorithm selection, key management architecture determines who can compel access to federal data: the agency, the CSP, or both. Government-managed keys, hardware security module (HSM) integration, and explicit key custody documentation are standard expectations at Moderate and above. Agencies that deploy to the cloud without validating their cryptographic module chain against FIPS requirements discover the gap during the Third Party Assessment Organization (3PAO) assessment, not before.
